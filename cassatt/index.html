A studio guide · Paris, 1878

Little Girl in a Blue Armchair, with a corner by Degas

Imagine you are Mary Cassatt, thirty-four, an American who has lived in Paris for four years, newly invited by Edgar Degas to show with the Impressionists. This guide follows how the picture was probably made: a child sprawled in a turquoise armchair, a room that Degas reshaped, and a dog that moved and moved back.

Cassatt's own letter of about 1903 says Degas advised her on the background and worked on it. The National Gallery of Art cleaned and studied the painting for its 2014 exhibition Degas/Cassatt, and infrared shows what changed. The materials have not been published. Each chapter opens with a note on what its evidence is. The slider steps through six stages; stages 1, 2, 3 and 5 are AI reconstructions.

I

Your training

EvidenceThe National Gallery of Art's 2014 exhibition brochure for the years in Paris; the dates of your schooling and Salons are from standard biographies and have not been checked against the archives.

You are Mary Cassatt, born in 1844 near Pittsburgh into a well-off banking family. You studied at the Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts in Philadelphia, and in 1866 went to Paris, where women could not enter the École des Beaux-Arts: you took private lessons and copied in the Louvre. You have also studied pictures in Italy and Spain.

From 1868 the Salon accepted your work in several years, but its jury grew harder to please: in 1875 it refused one of your two entries, and in 1877 both. You moved to France for good in 1874.

In 1877 Edgar Degas, ten years older, invites you to exhibit with the Impressionists, who call themselves independents. Your first sight of his art, you said later, “changed my life”. He is said to have said of your work, “there is someone who feels as I do.”

II

Join the independents

EvidenceThe National Gallery of Art's brochure and press release for Degas/Cassatt (2013–14), and an interview with its curator, Kimberly A. Jones.

You and Degas have much in common: both realists, drawn to people and modern life rather than landscape, both educated, witty and from banking families. Over the next decade you trade advice and try new materials together: distemper, metallic paint, prints.

He gives you more than an invitation. For this picture, he brings you the model, the child of friends of his. When you have painted her, he looks at the canvas, finds it good, advises you on the background, and then picks up a brush and works on it himself.

He will paint and draw you many times. He goes on to buy nearly a hundred of your works, more than he owned by any other living artist.

III

A child, a dog and four armchairs

EvidenceThe painting itself and the National Gallery of Art's collection page, which identifies the dog. Who the girl was is not known; where you painted the picture is not documented.

The model is a little girl, the daughter of friends of Degas. Her name has not come down to us. You let her slump: legs apart, one arm behind her head, eyes turned away, bored or tired. Your own dog sleeps on the next chair: Baptiste, a small Brussels griffon that Degas helped you find, who appears in several of your paintings.

You paint big for an intimate subject: the canvas is 130 centimetres wide. Seen from low down, at the child's level, the armchairs fill the picture and swallow her; the frame cuts them off on three sides.

A child as she is
Not posed or pretty: a real child, sprawling, with her dress rucked up.
Low and close
The viewpoint of someone sitting on the floor, with the furniture looming.
One colour
Four armchairs and a sofa in the same turquoise chintz, filling most of the canvas.

IV

Gather your materials

EvidenceNothing published: the analysis of this canvas's ground and pigments, if it was made, has not appeared in any source this guide could read. Its size and medium are from the National Gallery of Art.

Oil paint on a canvas 89.5 by 129.8 centimetres. Beyond that the record is silent. No study of this picture's canvas, ground or pigments has been published in anything this guide could read, so the layers below are only a schematic, and the colours on the palette are guesses from the surface.

What the surface does show: light colour throughout, quick loaded strokes in the upholstery, thin scumbled paint in the floor, and the pale, broken strokes of the lace. The National Gallery calls it, with its loose brushwork and light palette, “arguably” your first true Impressionist painting.

Not published

  • The canvas and its priming
  • Any drawing under the paint
  • The pigments, and the binding medium
  • What the X-rays show, beyond “changes beneath the paint surface”
  • The picture's history of varnish and repair before the latest cleaning

A 2014 essay by the conservator Ann Hoenigswald in the Degas/Cassatt catalogue, and an article by Kimberly A. Jones, may hold some of this; neither could be read for this guide.

V

Lay in the room

EvidenceInfrared images published by the National Gallery of Art in 2014 show the first line of the floor. How you began the canvas is otherwise not documented; stages 1 and 2 are plausible, not recorded.

  1. Place the furniture

    Big shapes first: the girl's armchair on the right, the dog's on the left, another armchair and an armless couch at the back.

  2. Set the girl in it

    Her body on a diagonal from the top of the chair to her feet at the bottom edge.

  3. Draw the floor's edge

    A single horizontal line, a little above her head, where the floor meets the back wall. The wall runs straight across, parallel to the canvas. Infrared still shows this line under the paint.

VI

Paint it, then share it

EvidenceYour letter to Vollard for the order; the National Gallery of Art's 2014 findings (magnification, infrared, X-rays) for Degas's corner, the couch and the dog. The dog's place on the floor is not shown precisely in any open source.

The order of the first two steps is in your own words: “I had done the child in the armchair”, and then Degas advised you on the background. The rest comes from the National Gallery's study.

1

The child in the armchair

Paint the girl first, sprawled in the big armchair: one arm behind her head, the other hanging over the arm of the chair, her legs apart, her eyes turned away. Her white dress is lace and loose strokes; her sash and socks are a tartan of green, black and blue, with orange and yellow lines.

Cover the armchairs with turquoise and a scatter of quick strokes in green, pink, red and white for the chintz pattern. Put Baptiste, your dog, asleep on the armchair at the left. Behind the furniture, the floor meets a single wall straight across the picture.

2

Degas's corner

Degas likes the picture and advises you on the background, and then he works on it himself. Where the floor met the back wall in one straight line, he paints a corner: two walls meeting, the floor's edge running away on a diagonal.

Under magnification his strokes are different from yours: greyish, almost silvery-brown, small, sharp and quick, found nowhere else in the picture. The surface here was also deliberately abraded, scraped or rubbed, a habit of his and not of yours.

3

Where to put the dog

The new corner changes the room, and you adjust to it. Rework the armless couch at the back so that it lines up with the sloping wall.

Try the dog somewhere else: infrared shows it once sat on the floor, in front of the reworked couch. Then paint it out and put it back where it was, on the armchair.

4

Sign it

Sign at lower left, in dark paint across the upholstery: Mary Cassatt. Later, someone writes the dealer's name and address in pencil on the stretcher: “M. Vollard / 6 Rue Lafitte”.

How the room changed

The lines below are redrawn by hand from the National Gallery's infrared diagram of 2014; the infrared image itself belongs to the Gallery and is not reproduced here. Their positions are approximate.

Laid over each other, the two rooms show what Degas did: one straight line becomes a corner. The room deepens; the floor opens out between the armchairs; the couch at the back, and the dog, have to find new places. Such wide-angle diagonals are common in his interiors, the National Gallery notes, and unprecedented in yours.

VII

Send it to the World's Fair, 1878

EvidenceYour letter to Vollard, about 1903, the only account of the refusal; no record of the jury's decision has been found for this guide.

In 1878 Paris holds a world's fair, the Exposition Universelle, on the Champ-de-Mars. Each country hangs its own art. You send this picture to the American section, and it is refused.

Twenty-five years later you are still angry. Degas had thought it good, you write to Vollard, and he had worked on it; it seemed new then; and the jury was three people, one of them a pharmacist.

The refusal comes a year after the Salon turned down both your entries. From 1879 you show with the independents, who have no jury. You remember the change later as a release.

VIII

What happened next

EvidenceThe National Gallery of Art's provenance and exhibition records; its 2013 press release, 2014 brochure and an interview with the curator on the study.

The fourth Impressionist exhibition opens in April 1879, and you make your debut with eleven works. The catalogue lists a Portrait de petite fille; the National Gallery records that it is possibly this picture.

You keep the canvas for a quarter of a century, then sell it to Vollard. In your letter to him you tell its story and underline that Degas worked on the background.

For a century nobody could say what he did. Before the 2014 exhibition the National Gallery's conservator Ann Hoenigswald cleaned it, and with the Gallery's scientists and the curator Kimberly A. Jones studied it under magnification, in infrared and in X-rays. They found his corner, your first floor line, the couch you reworked and the dog that left its chair and came back.

What is still not known

Who the girl was. Where you painted the picture, and over how long. What the canvas, ground and pigments are, and whether you drew on the canvas first. Exactly where the couch stood before you reworked it, and where the dog sat on the floor. Whether Degas did anything else to the picture. Whether it hung in the 1879 exhibition. The exact date and the French wording of your letter.

How Cassatt thought

  • Paint the child as she is. Slumped, bored and unposed, not a pretty portrait.
  • Get down to her level. A low viewpoint, with the furniture looming.
  • Let one colour carry it. Turquoise chintz across most of the canvas, in quick strokes.
  • Take advice, and adjust. Degas's corner accepted, the couch reworked, the dog tried and put back.
  • Trust your peers over juries. Refused in 1878; with the independents from 1879.
