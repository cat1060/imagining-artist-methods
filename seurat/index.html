A studio guide · Paris, 1884–89

A Sunday on La Grande Jatte, stroke by stroke and dot by dot

Imagine you are Georges Seurat, twenty-four, who left art school and has just finished his first huge canvas. This guide follows how the picture was probably made: months of drawings and small panels, a vast canvas painted in two campaigns a year apart, the dots added in the second, and a painted border three years later.

The Art Institute of Chicago's records, its 2004 exhibition Seurat and the Making of La Grande Jatte, and its 2022 study of the frame give the dates and the order of work. The technical studies by Inge Fiedler and Roy Berns could not be read for this guide and are known here only through summaries. Each chapter opens with a note on what its evidence is. I

Your training

EvidenceThe Art Institute of Chicago's 2004 exhibition texts and its 2022 article on the frame. Your reading of Chevreul and your visit to the 1879 exhibition are as the Art Institute reports them.

You are Georges Seurat, born in Paris in December 1859 into a middle-class family with enough income to support you for life. You trained at the École des Beaux-Arts under Henri Lehmann, a follower of Ingres, and left after a year and a half. After a year of military service you worked alone, mostly in black and white: conté crayon drawings of soft darks and lights.

At sixteen you read the colour theorist Michel-Eugène Chevreul: a colour looks stronger beside its complement, and colours set side by side mix in the eye differently from colours mixed on the palette. At twenty you saw the Impressionists' fourth exhibition, and their white frames left an “unexpectedly deep impression”.

Your first huge canvas, Bathing Place, Asnières, took thirteen studies. In it, men and boys look across the Seine to an island, a clump of green on the far bank: La Grande Jatte.

II

Choose a stage

EvidenceThe Art Institute of Chicago's 2004 exhibition texts for the setting, the cast, and the start date; your words to Gustave Kahn as the Art Institute quotes them in English.

From 22 May 1884 the island is your subject. Monet painted there in 1878; you choose the green park at its north-western tip, looking across the Seine to Courbevoie. The island's restaurants, dance halls, wine shops, and boatyards, and the factories across the river, stay out of the picture.

On it you will set a cast of modern Parisians on a Sunday: soldiers, boaters, the fashionable and the casually dressed, old and young, families, couples, and people alone. The Art Institute counts forty-eight people, three dogs, and eight boats. They will hardly look at one another.

III

Audition the cast

EvidenceThe Art Institute of Chicago's 2004 exhibition counts the studies, and a 2004 press review of it describes the changes to the woman in the pink skirt; the studies are in Chicago, New York, Washington, and elsewhere. Where each panel was painted is not recorded.

Before the big canvas, you make about twenty-eight drawings, twenty-eight small oil sketches on wood panels, and three larger canvases. The panels are about 16 by 25 centimetres; the drawings are in conté crayon on textured white paper.

They are auditions. You try out figures, move them, change them, and drop some. In one study a man approaches the woman in the pink skirt, and in the painting she leads a little girl instead. Others you keep from early on, like the woman walking a monkey, who already appears in a panel and in your final study.

The last of the canvases, now in New York, has almost the whole design, in small criss-cross strokes that one scholar compared to chopped straw. It is the best guide to how the big picture looked before the dots.

Figures turned sideways or straight on, like an Egyptian or Greek frieze.

IV

Gather your materials

EvidenceInge Fiedler's analyses of 1989 and 2004 for the Art Institute of Chicago, known here only through a summary by ColourLex; the size and medium are from the Art Institute's record.

Oil on a linen canvas 207.5 by 308.1 centimetres, now slightly out of square. The pigments found, as summarised from the Art Institute's analyses, are lead white, zinc yellow, chrome yellow, cadmium yellow, yellow ochre and iron oxide yellow, burnt sienna, vermilion, red lake, emerald green, viridian, cobalt blue, and artificial ultramarine, with black used rarely.

The palette changes between the two campaigns. In 1884–85 you still use earth colours and a little black. From October 1885 you drop them and keep to bright, spectral colours, adding zinc yellow and more chrome yellow, vermilion, and red lake.

Samples show lead white priming under parts of the paint, and in the sunlit grass, dots laid over a lower layer of chrome yellow, emerald green, and white that still shows through between them.

V

Begin the big canvas

EvidenceNothing documented about how the big canvas was begun. The Met's final study and the Art Institute's label describe the first campaign's brushwork; stages 1 to 3 are plausible, not recorded.

  1. Prepare the canvas

    A canvas over two metres high and three wide, with a pale priming. Its edges are tacked round the stretcher; four years later you will fold them out again.

  2. Set the stage

    The lawn sloping down to the river, the trees, and the long shadow across the foreground, from your studies of the park.

  3. Paint the cast

    The figures in small horizontal and criss-cross strokes of complementary colours, as in your final study. By March 1885 the picture is ready to show.

VI

Paint it twice

1

Lay in the park

Begin with the setting you have studied for months: the slope of the lawn, the river on the left, the trunks on the right, and the long shadows that cut across the foreground. Whether you painted the park first and the people after is not recorded.

Leave out what you saw and did not want: the cafés, dance halls, boatyards, and factories on and around the island.

2

Place the cast

Bring in the people you have tried out in your drawings and panels: forty-eight of them, with three dogs and eight boats. Stand them in profile or full face, stiff and still, like figures in a frieze.

Paint them, as the rest, in small strokes of pure colour laid side by side, mostly horizontal or criss-crossed, the manner of your final study. You plan to show the picture in March 1885; the exhibition is cancelled, and you put the canvas aside.

3

Dots, dabs, and outlines

In October 1885 you come back to the canvas with a new method. Over the first strokes you lay small dabs, dashes, and dots of colour, each next to its complement, to mix in the eye rather than on the palette.

You change the figures too. Starker, more columnar shapes get scalloped and curved outlines; some silhouettes grow, such as the bustle of the woman with the parasol at the right, and a few figures are added. The Art Institute names the monkey on its leash and a man with a rolled newspaper far in the distance, though a monkey is already in your studies of 1884.

4

Paint a border

In 1888–89, after your picture Models, you start to rework the edges of your finished canvases. You take this one off its stretcher, fold out the tacking margins, prime them with lead white, and restretch it larger.

On the new strip, about three centimetres wide, paint a border of red, orange, and blue dots, a bridge between the scene and your white frame. The width is measured on a photograph.

Why dots

Colours mixed on the palette grow duller; colours laid side by side in small touches are mixed by the eye and keep their brightness, you believe, and each colour is heightened by its complement beside it. Seen from a distance, the dots fuse into solid, glowing forms; seen close, the figures dissolve. Critics called the method pointillism. You preferred other names: divisionism, or chromo-luminarism.

The dots also took time. The picture you had finished by March 1885 took another winter to repaint.

VII

Show it, May 1886

EvidenceThe Art Institute of Chicago's exhibition history and 2004 texts; its 2022 article on the frame, which says no photographs of these exhibitions have been found.

The eighth and last Impressionist exhibition opens on 15 May 1886 at 1 rue Laffitte. Camille Pissarro has invited you; with his son Lucien and your friend Paul Signac you are hung in a room of your own, apart from Degas, Gauguin, and the rest.

You are twenty-six. Your picture is number 175 in the catalogue, and the most notorious single canvas in the show. Most critics ignore or mock it; a few young writers in literary journals champion it, and the new direction is soon baptized Neo-Impressionism. In August it goes on to the Indépendants, and in February 1887 to Les XX in Brussels.

It hangs in a white frame. No one photographed it, but visitors mention white frames, and you painted the picture, borderless and in a broad white frame, on the wall behind your Models.

VIII

What happened next

EvidenceThe Art Institute of Chicago's record (provenance and exhibitions), its 2022 article on the frame, and Roy Berns's popular account of the darkening (2007). The comparison of the grass is a rough recolouring made for this guide, not Berns's simulation.

Two years after the show you paint Models and begin to rework the edges of your finished canvases. This one comes off its stretcher; its tacking margins are folded out and primed with lead white, and on them you paint the border. The picture grows by a few centimetres on every side, and its old white frame, too small now, is probably lost.

Some of your bright colours did not last. The zinc yellow of the second campaign was unstable: within a few years, it turned from lemon yellow to ochre and brown. Félix Fénéon noticed in 1892. Dots that were meant as points of light became dark holes, and the sunlit yellow-greens went olive. In 2006 the colour scientist Roy Berns, with the Art Institute's scientists, published a digital simulation of the colours before the change.

How Seurat thought

  • Plan it like a play. A stage, a cast, and auditions in drawings and panels before the big canvas.
  • Let the eye do the mixing. Pure colours side by side, each beside its complement.
  • Make the moment permanent. Modern people standing still, like a frieze.
  • Go back and rework. A second campaign a year on, and a border three years later.
  • Design the edge too. A painted border, then a white frame, as part of the picture.
