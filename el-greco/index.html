A studio guide · Toledo, about 1580

Christ Carrying the Cross, layer by layer

Imagine you are El Greco, a Cretan painter trained in Venice, newly settled in Toledo and about to paint a Christ who embraces his cross instead of buckling under it. This guide follows what your process would have been, from a reddish ground to the last wet light in the eyes.

This canvas, probably your first version, has no published technical study, so the materials and layers come from a 2014 study of another version in the Thyssen-Bornemisza museum and from your practice generally; the Met's records describe this canvas's condition. Each chapter opens with a note on what its evidence is. The slider steps through nine stages; all but the last are AI reconstructions.

I

From icon to Toledo

EvidenceThe Vatican Museums' biography, the Met's essay and the Lehman catalogue.

You are Domenikos Theotokopoulos, born in Candia, on Crete, in 1541, and a master icon painter by 1563. Two icons by you are certain, a Dormition and a Saint Luke.

In 1567 you go to Venice, where you absorb Titian, Tintoretto, Veronese and Jacopo Bassano. In Rome in 1570 the miniaturist Giulio Clovio introduces you to Cardinal Alessandro Farnese as a disciple of Titian, which is his word, not proof of an apprenticeship. In 1572 you join the painters' guild in Rome and open a workshop.

In 1576 you are in Madrid, and from 1577 in Toledo, where you paint the Espolio for the cathedral and the altarpieces of Santo Domingo el Antiguo. You will die there on 7 April 1614.

II

A new Christ

EvidenceJonathan Brown's catalogue entry (1998) and the Met. The influence of Saint Teresa's followers is a hypothesis.

Spanish patrons know Christ carrying the cross from Sebastiano del Piombo: a Christ bowed under the weight. A version on slate in the Escorial was especially dear to Philip II, and the Spanish painter Luis de Morales repeated the type.

You change it. Your Christ stands upright, embraces the cross and looks up to heaven, so that the cross becomes, in Brown's phrase, a medium of salvation rather than a burden.

For devotion
A picture for private prayer, close and half-length. The type was very popular with your clients. The first owner of this one is unknown.
The embrace
Long hands wrapped around the wood, the cross held rather than dragged.
The gaze
Eyes raised and wet with tears, the head turned up toward the light.
The sky
A stormy grey-blue sky, lit around the head.

This canvas is probably your first version, painted about 1580–85, though scholars' dates for it range more widely.

III

Run a workshop

EvidenceFrancisco Pacheco's account of his visit in 1611 (published 1649), the inventories of 1614 and 1621, and Wethey's catalogue as summarised by Brown.

A successful design gets repeated. When the painter Francisco Pacheco visited in 1611, he saw small canvases of the originals of everything you had painted, kept as models, and clay, wax and plaster figures you used for poses. His account is first-hand but critical.

Assistants help. Your Italian assistant Francisco Preboste worked with you from about 1596 to 1607 and acted as witness and agent; your son Jorge Manuel joined you, and Luis Tristán trained with you from 1603 to 1607.

The inventory of 1621 lists canvases as prepared, sketched in, begun and finished, which is documentary support for showing your work in stages. One count gives eleven versions of this subject partly or wholly by you, and ten by followers.

IV

Gather your materials

EvidenceThe Thyssen-Bornemisza's 2014 study of its version and of its other El Grecos. This canvas has not been analysed.

For the version now in the Thyssen-Bornemisza, conservators found a gesso and glue ground under a reddish-brown primer of red ochre, lead white, umber and black in linseed oil, with scrapings from your palette mixed in. The flesh was bound in walnut oil.

Across your Spanish works they found smalt, azurite, lapis lazuli, verdigris and copper resinate, ochres, minium, vermilion, carmine, charcoal and bone black. For this canvas, every card below is a likelihood.

Tools

  • Stiff, thick-bristled brushes to drag the lights in impasto.
  • Softer brushes for the glazes laid over them.
  • A slab and muller to grind each colour in oil.

V

Ground and lay-in

EvidenceThe Thyssen study (infrared and X-ray), the Met's notes on another of its El Grecos, and your practice generally. Inferred for this canvas.

  1. Gesso

    Glue-sized linen with a thin white ground. Another of your paintings at the Met has a thin white ground under a reddish-brown layer.

  2. Reddish primer

    In Italy your grounds were white or light grey; in Spain they turn reddish-brown, and you leave them showing where the paint is thin.

  3. A few lines

    Your gridded Italian underdrawing has all but disappeared. In infrared, the Thyssen version shows only a few faint lines for the head, curls, hand and tunic.

  4. Correct as you go

    In the Thyssen version you altered the neck, curls, fingers and ear, and raised the gaze, which first looked to the left. No changes are published for this canvas.

VI

Build the figure

EvidenceThe Thyssen study and your practice generally; the order of steps here is inferred for this canvas.

You build up layers rich in lead white, pure white in the brightest lights, and then glaze what you want to stress. Each stage needs time to dry, so the picture takes several sessions.

1

Sky and cross

Stain the background first, leaving a space for the figure: grey-blue storm clouds, darker toward the edges. In the Thyssen version, early base tones under the cross and the dark sky seem to have darkened the primer there.

Block in the cross in dark brown, the base for the light edges you will add later.

2

Drapery masses

Lay in the blue cloak over the shoulder and the crimson tunic as broad, flat masses, without folds yet. The colours here are not analysed; azurite or smalt for the blue and vermilion with red lake for the red are likely.

3

Flesh

Paint the face, neck and hands in thin, warm lead white. In the Thyssen version the flesh of the hand is lead white with vermilion, lead-tin yellow and carmine, bound in walnut oil.

Let the reddish primer show through in the half-tones. It does the work of the shadows.

4

Modelling and glazes

Now model the folds. Drag stiff, thick-bristled brushes through paint rich in lead white for the lights, then glaze over what you want to deepen.

Catch the light along the edges of the cross. Your brushwork stays visible; you never blend it smooth.

5

Hair, beard and thorns

Paint the curls and the beard, then the crown of thorns, crisp and sharp-pointed against the hair, with drops of blood on the brow.

6

Final lights

Last, the brightest lights: pure white on the brow, the nose and the knuckles, and white and crimson touches in the eyes so they look wet with tears. In the Thyssen version these touches are what give the eyes their shine.

VII

Last touches

EvidenceThe inscription on this canvas; Pacheco's criticism, a hostile witness.

Sign it on the cross above Christ's left hand, in cursive Greek: doménikos theotokópoulos epoíei, Domenikos Theotokopoulos made this. The verb is in the imperfect, “was making”, an old formula that leaves the work open. The signature is now partly worn.

Not everyone admires your finish. Pacheco faulted your habit of retouching again and again and leaving colours unblended, so that your pictures looked unfinished. He meant it as criticism; it describes what you chose to do.

VIII

What time has changed

EvidenceThe Met's catalogue (1998) and its photographs of the canvas.

  • Its owners: the conde de Quinto, then the Stirling Maxwell collection, then Robert Lehman, who bought it in 1953 or 1954. It came to the Met with his collection in 1975.
  • It was lined in the past with a water-based adhesive, which flattened the texture of your brushwork.
  • There is a sizable repair in the blue cloak.
  • In 1978 it was lightly cleaned, lifting paint was set down, and losses in the background and at the edges were retouched.
  • The state of the varnish has not been published.

How El Greco thought

  • Rethink the familiar. A Christ who embraces the cross instead of buckling under it.
  • Start from colour. A reddish ground that warms everything and stands in for the shadows.
  • Draw with the brush. A few lines, then corrections in paint, not a careful drawing underneath.
  • Let the stroke show. Dragged lights and open brushwork that critics called unfinished.
  • Make it again, better. Keep small replicas, and refine the design version after version.
